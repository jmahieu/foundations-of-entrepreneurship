When do people become self-employed?

These are all men who become self-employed between age 20 and 43. What do you notice?

Source: Humphries, J. E. (2017). The causes and consequences of self-employment over the life cycle. Doctoral dissertation, The University of Chicago. Figure shows the career states of Swedish males born in 1970 who are ever self-employed between 1990 and 2013.

Most future entrepreneurs are employees first

  • At ages 20–25, most people in the sample are still in school or paid employment
  • Paid employment remains the largest career state throughout the observed age range
  • Incorporated and unincorporated self-employment become more common as the cohort gets older
  • But entrepreneurship is also transitory: only 45–50% of those who ever became self-employed are still so by age 43
  • Entrepreneurship therefore often looks less like a first job and more like a career transition

Source: Humphries (2017), Figure 2.

Successful founders are usually middle-aged too

Recall the figure from the previous lecture:

  • The share of all U.S. startup founders peaks around age 40
  • The founders of the fastest-growing ventures are older still
  • For the 1-in-1,000 fastest-growing ventures, mean founder age is 45

The popular image of the 20-year-old founder is memorable but not representative.

Azoulay, P., Jones, B. F., Kim, J. D., & Miranda, J. (2020). Age and high-growth entrepreneurship. American Economic Review: Insights, 2(1), 65–82.

Entrepreneurs usually come from organizations

  • New ventures are rarely founded by people arriving directly from school or from outside the labor market
  • Reviews of the evidence suggest that at least nine in ten entrepreneurs have previously worked for another organization
  • Their current employer is therefore part of the entrepreneurial process, even before a new firm exists

Beliefs and valuesDemystify entrepreneurship, reduce stigma, spur a taste for autonomy

The same employer may simultaneously prepare people to leave and give them reasons to stay.

Sørensen, J. B., & Fassiotto, M. A. (2011). Organizations as fonts of entrepreneurship. Organization Science, 22(5), 1322–1331.

The Traitorous Eight

Eight employees who left Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory in 1957—only two years after its founding—to found Fairchild Semiconductor. This eventually led to numerous spinoffs, including AMD and Intel. Over 20 years, 65 different companies were started by 1st- or 2nd-generation teams that traced their Silicon Valley origins to Shockley Semiconductor. In 2014, an estimated 92 of 130 listed descendant firms were together worth over US$2.1 trillion.

Why did so much entrepreneurship emerge from one organizational community?

  • Did Shockley select unusually entrepreneurial people?
  • Did working there develop valuable capabilities?
  • Did alumni keep helping one another through a powerful network?

The young and talented?

  • Keep in mind that Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory was still a small startup: two years old, about 30 employees
  • How could Shockley attract these highly able young scientists (6 of the 8 had a PhD) with only the few resources he had? Can this in some way also explain their eventual exit to found their own company?

“Talented people don’t need to work for you; they have plenty of options. You should ask yourself a more pointed question: Why would someone join your company as its 20th engineer when she could work at Google for more money and more prestige?”

Peter Thiel (2014), Zero to One, p. 20

Roach, M., & Sauermann, H. (2024). Can technology startups hire talented early employees? Ability, preferences, and employee first job choice. Management Science, 70(6), 3619–3644.

Ability and preferences

Roach, M., & Sauermann, H. (2024). Can technology startups hire talented early employees? Ability, preferences, and employee first job choice. Management Science, 70(6), 3619–3644.

Also to bypass the career ladder?

  • Working in a startup like Shockley Semiconductor Lab also gave these young scientists exposure to tasks normally reserved for more experienced workers
  • (Inadvertently) prepared them for entrepreneurship? Entrepreneurs need to be creative (inventive) but also need business skills
  • Creativity peaks when you are young
  • Business acumen increases with experience in high-level positions
  • This can also explain the “inverted-U” relation between entrepreneurship and age (see previous class)
  • Implication: having too many older workers in society slows entrepreneurship—older workers occupy key positions and block skill acquisition by young people

Liang, J., Wang, H., & Lazear, E. P. (2018). Demographics and entrepreneurship. Journal of Political Economy, 126(S1), S140–S196.

Tilting the ability–preferences trade-off

  • While Shockley was a brilliant scientist—he won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1956—he was a terrible manager. Instead of inspiring and motivating his hires, he would gradually undermine them and sap their confidence.

“When he hired you, you were the greatest person in the world. Then slowly you worked your way down the line. First you were brilliant. Then, ‘You’re doing a good job.’ Then, ‘You’re capable, but I’m unsure about you… Now I’m really unsure… Now I think you’re inadequate. I don’t think you can do the job for me.’ He kept a black book on everybody.”

Sheldon Roberts, Los Angeles Times (2001)
  • Where before preferences > opportunity cost of working elsewhere, this now shifted—because of the lack of actual challenge, autonomy and creativity
  • This will particularly affect the most able employees, because for them the trade-off is the steepest

Who leaves? Where to? Why worry?

  • The relative bargaining power of firm and employee depends on whether the firm’s complementary assets (IP, physical capital, reputation, routines, tacit knowledge) are important for value creation and whether the employee can walk away with them—or recreate them cheaply after exit
  • High-ability workers benefit most from these assets, but are also the ones who can most easily recreate them. Shockley thought he was in the “firm advantage” bracket whereas there was actually an “employee advantage”
  • When these high performers leave, they are more likely to found a new company instead of moving to another firm—and to compete with the parent firm

Campbell, B. A., Ganco, M., Franco, A. M., & Agarwal, R. (2012). Who leaves, where to, and why worry? Employee mobility, entrepreneurship and effects on source firm performance. Strategic Management Journal, 33(1), 65–87.

Why people leave also matters

  • Lee et al. (2024) find that founders who leave their parent firm due to organizational misalignment motives (frustration with bureaucracy, strategic disagreement, …) tend to found successful initial ventures, whereas founders who leave for other reasons rarely do
  • This is because founders with organizational misalignment motives engage in “venture crafting”—carefully considering and implementing organizational activities to build well-functioning organizations, so that the issues that colored their experiences at their parent firms do not derail their ventures
  • Put differently: founders acted deliberately to ensure their ventures were organized differently from the firms they had chosen to leave, and chose not to carry certain practices forward

“[…] the idea that you needed something in addition to just scaling the models up, which is alignment or safety. You don’t tell the models what their values are just by pouring more compute into them. And so there were a set of people who believed in those two ideas. We really trusted each other and wanted to work together. And so we went off and started our own company with that idea in mind.”

Dario Amodei, on leaving OpenAI to found Anthropic

Lee, H., Shah, S. K., & Agarwal, R. (2024). Spinning an entrepreneurial career: Motivation, attribution, and the development of organizational capabilities. Strategic Management Journal, 45(3), 463–506.

Does bureaucracy breed entrepreneurship?

If changes in job design and amenities (more routine work, less challenging tasks, top-down decision making) and organizational misalignment drive people to consider entrepreneurship and craft better ventures, shouldn’t we expect many entrepreneurs to come from large, bureaucratic organizations?

The Fairchild view Young entrepreneurial firms
  • Learn about the entrepreneurial process
  • Exposure to a network of suppliers of labor, goods, capital & customers
  • Self-selection on risk
The Xerox view Large bureaucratic firms
  • Incapable of responding to radical innovations that upset established ways of organizing (inertia)
  • Incapable of evaluating employees’ ideas as they fall outside the core business (myopia)
  • Focus on the core business (the diversification discount)

Which “view” is more prevalent?

Gompers, P., Lerner, J., & Scharfstein, D. (2005). Entrepreneurial spawning: Public corporations and the genesis of new ventures, 1986 to 1999. The Journal of Finance, 60(2), 577–614.

Bureaucratic employers spawn fewer entrepreneurs

Using Danish employer–employee data, Sørensen (2007) finds:

  • Employees of larger and older organizations are less likely to enter entrepreneurship
  • True for (individual) self-employment and for larger startups (multiple joiners in the first year)

Sørensen, J. B. (2007). Bureaucracy and entrepreneurship: Workplace effects on entrepreneurial entry. Administrative Science Quarterly, 52(3), 387–412.

Small firms generate disproportionate numbers of founders

Elfenbein et al. (2010) study U.S. scientists and engineers:

  • Employees of small firms are much more likely to transition into self-employment
  • For employees in firms with 1–25 workers, entry is more than three times the sample average
  • Prior small-firm employment is also associated with stronger early entrepreneurial outcomes

This is the small-firm effect.

Elfenbein, D. W., Hamilton, B. H., & Zenger, T. R. (2010). The small firm effect and the entrepreneurial spawning of scientists and engineers. Management Science, 56(4), 659–681.

One small-firm pattern, four possible explanations

ExplanationMechanismMatrix location
Preference sorting People who value autonomy choose small firms and later entrepreneurship Selection / neutral
Ability sorting High-ability workers prefer stronger pay-for-performance and later exploit their ability Selection / potentially enhancing
Opportunity cost Workers with less to lose leave paid work more readily Selection / neutral
Entrepreneurial human capital Broad tasks and proximity to founders teach workers how to build firms Treatment / enhancing

Elfenbein et al. find evidence consistent with several mechanisms—not one pure treatment effect.

Firm closure removes the internal career option

Kacperczyk & Marx revisit the small-firm effect in the automatic speech-recognition industry:

  • Among continuing firms, smaller employers spawn more entrepreneurs
  • At firm dissolution, internal opportunities disappear for everyone
  • The relationship reverses: employees leaving larger dissolved firms are more likely to become entrepreneurs
  • A one-standard-deviation increase in logged firm size is associated with about 15% higher odds of entrepreneurship in the dissolution sample
    • If 5 in 100 leavers from an average-sized dissolved firm found a venture, then among leavers from a firm one SD larger it is roughly 5.7 in 100

Kacperczyk, A., & Marx, M. (2016). Revisiting the small-firm effect on entrepreneurship: Evidence from firm dissolutions. Organization Science, 27(4), 893–910.

What do workers learn?

Chatterji (2009) studies new medical-device ventures:

  • Spawns founded by former employees of industry incumbents outperform other entrants (higher valuations, shorter time to funding)
  • Their advantage does not appear to come mainly from copying the parent’s patented technology
  • Interviews instead emphasize regulatory strategy, reimbursement, marketing and managerial knowledge

However: talented employees may select into strong parent firms and later into entrepreneurship.

The idea is often discovered at work

From a nationally representative survey in the US (so both growth and subsistence entrepreneurship):

  • Most business ideas (40%) are “discovered” at work, next to personal use of a product
  • Customer problems, technologies, market gaps and rejected internal projects can all become entrepreneurial opportunities
  • Employment does more than pay a wage—it changes what people are able to notice

Bennett, V. M., & Chatterji, A. K. (2017). The entrepreneurial process: Evidence from a nationally representative survey. Working Paper.

Professional experience also shapes which ideas you discover

  • Shane (2000) looked at startups that exploited 3D printing, invented at MIT. Despite the technology being well known at the time (patent applications, publications, industry consortium meetings), only eight applications were identified by the end of the 1990s
  • None of these applications were obvious from knowledge about the technology alone

“All of the 3DP lab’s work was in one ceramic powder and one fluid, and medical applications require multiple fluids and multiple powders, specifically polymers. You couldn’t just look at the 3DP process and know that you could use it for medical purposes. To make use of the 3DP process for drug delivery, you had to know something about what drugs and drug delivery systems are made from and how drug manufacture operates.”

Walter Flamenbaum — Therics

Shane, S. (2000). Prior knowledge and the discovery of entrepreneurial opportunities. Organization Science, 11(4), 448–469.

Recognition, not search

  • The eight entrepreneurs described a discovery process that involved recognition, rather than a search for information. In none of the cases did an entrepreneur indicate that he or she was searching for the opportunity prior to its discovery
  • Entrepreneurs are not better than others at searching for and discovering opportunities in general

“If Bob Cohn, who has been responsible for drug delivery systems and product development at J&J had looked at it, it would have been different… [H]e would have turned around and said, ‘holy cow!’ This is a platform that’s wonderful for drug delivery systems. If you had showed it to someone who does tissue engineering, they would have said, ‘holy cow!’ This is wonderful for tissue healing matrices for our biological development of tissues and organs. It’s a matter of mind set and background.”

Walter Flamenbaum — Therics

Recap: organizations as fonts of entrepreneurship

Where you work and what you do influences your likelihood of becoming an entrepreneur—and how successful you will be. There are important trade-offs:

Large, innovative firm. Provides many resources and new technologies, but these companies are very good at retaining you—voluntarily through high opportunity costs, or involuntarily through non-competes, for example.
Large, bureaucratic firm. Offers fewer resources and less skill development useful in entrepreneurship, but these firms will care less if you want to spin off.
Small firm. Exposes you to many of the tasks entrepreneurs do, but you are less likely to be exposed to the technological frontier, and the chance of involuntary exit through firm failure is higher.

Opportunity recognition has social sources

Ozgen & Baron (2007) study three sources of information:

  • Mentors help interpret experience and build useful cognitive frameworks (“schemas”)
  • Professional forums expose people to varied information and analogies
  • Informal industry contacts reveal problems, practices and changing demand

These sources are positively associated with recognizing more opportunities.

Ozgen, E., & Baron, R. A. (2007). Social sources of information in opportunity recognition: Effects of mentors, industry networks, and professional forums. Journal of Business Venturing, 22(2), 174–192.

The importance of schemas: opportunity recognition as pattern recognition

  • Experienced entrepreneurs have a more clearly defined prototype (schema) of an opportunity
  • Opportunity prototypes of experienced entrepreneurs are richer in content (they contain more dimensions)
  • They focus more on factors related to starting and running new ventures (see table)
  • Mentors might substitute for such entrepreneurial experience

Baron, R. A., & Ensley, M. D. (2006). Opportunity recognition as the detection of meaningful patterns: Evidence from comparisons of novice and experienced entrepreneurs. Management Science, 52(9), 1331–1344.

Entrepreneurship can spread through peers

Two settings show social transmission of entrepreneurial entry:

Workplace peers Nanda & Sørensen (2010)
  • Employees are more likely to become entrepreneurs when coworkers have prior entrepreneurial experience
  • The association is strongest when people have less entrepreneurial exposure elsewhere
  • Patterns are consistent with information and practical know-how, as well as role models and demystifying failure
University peers Kacperczyk (2013)
  • Among hedge-fund professionals, prior entrepreneurship among university peers predicts entry
  • Effects are stronger for nearby and same-gender peers
  • Consistent with information exchange and social referencing that reduces uncertainty

But neither study observes the mediators directly or isolates one mechanism.

Nanda, R., & Sørensen, J. B. (2010). Workplace peers and entrepreneurship. Management Science, 56(7), 1116–1126.

Kacperczyk, A. J. (2013). Social influence and entrepreneurship: The effect of university peers on entrepreneurial entry. Organization Science, 24(3), 664–683.

Informed peers may talk you out of a bad startup

Lerner & Malmendier exploit the assignment of Harvard MBA students to sections:

  • Sections differed in the share of classmates with prior entrepreneurial experience
  • More experienced entrepreneurial peers reduced later entrepreneurial entry
  • The reduction came from fewer unsuccessful ventures
  • The effect on successful ventures was substantially more positive

Better information can improve selection by preventing weak entry.

Lerner, J., & Malmendier, U. (2013). With a little help from my (random) friends: Success and failure in post-business school entrepreneurship. Review of Financial Studies, 26(10), 2411–2452.

Advice quality matters more than advice quantity

Chatterji et al. (2019) randomly matched 100 Indian technology startups with experienced peer advisers:

  • Advisers differed in whether they used active, formal people management—regular meetings, goals and feedback
  • Advice from active managers produced ventures that were 28% larger after two years
  • These ventures were 10 percentage points less likely to fail
  • Founders with an MBA or incubator/accelerator experience did not show the same response

Chatterji, A., Delecourt, S., Hasan, S., & Koning, R. (2019). When does advice impact startup performance? Strategic Management Journal, 40(3), 331–356.

Repeated peer interaction can improve firm performance

Cai & Szeidl randomized 2,820 young Chinese firms:

  • Treated owner-managers met monthly in ten-person business groups for one year
  • Meetings increased firm revenue by 8.1% during the intervention
  • Effects persisted one year after the meetings ended
  • Profits, management practices, borrowing, clients and suppliers also improved

Additional interventions point to peer learning and supplier–customer matching as mechanisms.

Cai, J., & Szeidl, A. (2018). Interfirm relationships and business performance. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 133(3), 1229–1282.

Incubators are associated with more entrepreneurship

Lyons & Zhang evaluate a Canadian technology-entrepreneurship program combining education, mentors, cohort ties, investors and potential seed capital:

  • They compare 335 accepted participants with unaccepted applicants who reached the finalist stage
  • Program participation is associated with +24 percentage points in later startup-related activity
  • …and +23 percentage points in later startup founding
  • …plus stronger venture survival, funding and employment outcomes

Lyons, E., & Zhang, L. (2018). Who does (not) benefit from entrepreneurship programs? Strategic Management Journal, 39(1), 85–112.

The largest gains appear among technology-founding novices

For applicants without prior entrepreneurship, participation is associated with approximately:

  • +41 percentage points in later entrepreneurial activity
  • +38 percentage points in later startup founding
  • Prior entrepreneurial experience reduces these estimated relationships by about 33 and 30 percentage points
  • The reduction is concentrated among applicants who had already founded a technology company

Lyons, E., & Zhang, L. (2018). Who does (not) benefit from entrepreneurship programs? Strategic Management Journal, 39(1), 85–112. Figure recreated from Table 6.

Cash and workspace are not the same as entrepreneurial learning

Gonzalez-Uribe & Leatherbee study Start-Up Chile using score cutoffs:

  • Basic services combined cash and coworking space → no detectable effect on fundraising, employment or survival for ventures near the admission cutoff
  • Entrepreneurship schooling bundled with those services → increased follow-on finance and venture scale

Structured learning—not the incubator label by itself—appears to be the active treatment.

Gonzalez-Uribe, J., & Leatherbee, M. (2018). The effects of business accelerators on venture performance: Evidence from Start-Up Chile. Review of Financial Studies, 31(4), 1566–1603. Figure recreated from Tables 6 and 7.

Some accelerators accelerate learning; others do not

Hallen, Cohen & Bingham compare accepted and “almost accepted” ventures across early accelerators:

  • Some programs substantially accelerate venture development
  • Other programs show little evidence of doing so
  • Sorting and signaling explain part—but not all—of the observed differences

Their qualitative evidence identifies a distinctive learning mechanism: Broad, intensive and paced consultation

Incubation can help disadvantaged entrepreneurs scale

Assenova studies a South African incubator for socially and educationally disadvantaged entrepreneurs:

  • Incubated firms are compared with similar, also-selected but non-incubated firms
  • Over six months, incubated firms grew 22% more in revenue
  • They also grew 15% more in employment
  • The pattern suggests that incubation can help close gaps in prior knowledge and access

Assenova, V. A. (2020). Early-stage venture incubation and mentoring promote learning, scaling, and profitability among disadvantaged entrepreneurs. Organization Science, 31(6), 1560–1578. Figure recreated from Table 1.

Mentor quality matters most when prior knowledge is scarce

Within the same incubator, participants were randomly assigned to mentors:

  • Assignment to a high-ability rather than low-ability mentor produced 3.2% higher revenue one year later
  • Profits were 3.5% higher
  • Mentoring generated more learning and larger performance improvements for founders with less prior knowledge and experience

The value of advice depends on the match between what the mentor provides and what the founder lacks.

Assenova, V. A. (2020). Early-stage venture incubation and mentoring promote learning, scaling, and profitability among disadvantaged entrepreneurs. Organization Science, 31(6), 1560–1578. Figure recreated from Table 3, Model (9).
