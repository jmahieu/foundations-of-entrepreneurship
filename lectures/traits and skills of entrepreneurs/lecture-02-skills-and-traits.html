The skills and traits of entrepreneurs

Lecture 2
Jeroen Mahieu

The Investment Choice

You have €100,000 to invest for 10 years. Which do you choose?

Option A Start your own company

You put most of your wealth into one private firm where you also work.

  • About 35% chance the business survives the first 10 years
  • About 10% chance you lose everything you invested
  • Median capital gain: 6.9% per year
  • Upper quartile: 18.6% per year
  • About 10% chance of >200% excess gains per year vs. the public stock market
Option B Invest in the public stock market

You buy a diversified public equity index. Over comparable horizons, public equity returns are:

  • Minimum: 5.6% per year
  • Maximum: 19.9% per year
  • Median excess gains compared to entrepreneurs: 2% per year

The questions these findings raise

  • Clearly, it is hard to explain why many people become entrepreneurs based on expected financial returns alone.
  • Who becomes an entrepreneur, and why?
  • What are the influences of particular personal characteristics and environmental factors?

Possible explanations

Preferences / traits-based Who is willing to enter
  • Entrepreneurs “pay” for certain amenities (compensating differentials)
  • Entrepreneurs like risk (and/or skewness)
  • Entrepreneurs are too optimistic about the future
  • Entrepreneurs are too confident about themselves or their projects
Skills-based Who actually performs better
  • Entrepreneurship rewards ability differently than wage work
  • Entrepreneurs have a high portion of “entrepreneurial” human capital
  • Entrepreneurs have a valuable social network
?

Before we examine these questions, we first need to know…

Are entrepreneurs born or made?

Nature vs. Nurture

  • We have long known that having an entrepreneurial parent raises your odds of becoming an entrepreneur yourself.
  • Is this selection (genes) or treatment (parental interactions)?
  • By comparing adopted vs. non-adopted children, we can tease apart these influences.

Nature vs. Nurture

  • Lindquist et al. (2015): having entrepreneurial (biological) parents raises the likelihood of becoming an entrepreneur by 45–65%. Two-thirds of this effect is nurture, one-third nature.
  • Vladasel et al. (2021): family influence matters more for growth-oriented (incorporated) than unincorporated entrepreneurs, with genes accounting for roughly half of this effect.

Lindquist, M. J., Sol, J., & Van Praag, M. (2015). Why do entrepreneurial parents have entrepreneurial children? Journal of Labor Economics, 33(2), 269–296.

Vladasel, T., Lindquist, M. J., Sol, J., & Van Praag, M. (2021). On the origins of entrepreneurship: Evidence from sibling correlations. Journal of Business Venturing, 36(5), 106017.

Preferences

Explanation 1: Paying for what the job gives you

  • Compensating differentials: if entrepreneurs earn less on average (Hamilton 2000 — ~35% lower median earnings after 10 years), they must be receiving something else in return.
  • That “something else” is non-pecuniary: autonomy, flexibility, being your own boss, building something of your own, doing work you find meaningful.
  • The financial “penalty” is then not a puzzle — it is the price of a job you like.

Hamilton, B. H. (2000). Does entrepreneurship pay? An empirical analysis of the returns to self-employment. Journal of Political Economy, 108(3), 604–631.

Compensating Differentials (Rosen, 1974; 1986)

Source: Lavetti, Kurt. 2023. “Compensating Wage Differentials in Labor Markets: Empirical Challenges and Applications.” Journal of Economic Perspectives, 37(3): 189–212.

Why do people actually start businesses?

  • Hurst & Pugsley (2011): most founders are not would-be high-growth innovators. Asked why they started, non-pecuniary reasons dominate (flexibility, being one’s own boss).
  • Most small-business owners do not want to grow and do not plan to innovate — plumbers, dentists, consultants, shopkeepers.
  • “Having a good business idea” is cited by only about a third of founders.

Hurst, E., & Pugsley, B. W. (2011). What do small businesses do? Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, Fall 2011, 73–142. (Shares approximate.)

Autonomy is worth paying for

  • Benz & Frey (2008): the self-employed report higher job satisfaction than employees — even after holding income and hours constant.
  • This is procedural utility: people value not just what they get, but how the outcome comes about — being self-determined rather than told what to do.
  • A concrete estimate of the compensating differential: people would give up meaningful income to keep the autonomy of working for themselves.

Benz, M., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Being independent is a great thing: Subjective evaluations of self-employment and hierarchy. Economica, 75(298), 362–383.

Not only the Moms and Pops

  • Tech-startup joiners value independence and challenge more than employees of established small or large firms.
  • This “fire in the belly” is linked to a higher rate of innovation by startup employees.

Sauermann, H. (2018). Fire in the belly? Employee motives and innovative performance in start‑ups versus established firms. Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal, 12(4), 423–454.

Explanation 2: Risk Tolerance

  • Entrepreneurs are commonly portrayed as risk takers — so they may be “okay” with lower-than-expected returns, explaining part of the puzzle.
  • What does the evidence say?
  • Cramer et al. (2002) measured risk aversion via willingness to pay for a lottery ticket (10 tickets, single prize of 1,000 guilders). Conditional on wealth, this reservation price reflects risk aversion.
  • Entrepreneurs are indeed less risk averse — but risk aversion is also highly correlated with (ex-post) wealth. Do risk-tolerant people become wealthier, or do the wealthy become risk-tolerant?

Cramer, J. S., Hartog, J., Jonker, N., & Van Praag, C. M. (2002). Low risk aversion encourages the choice for entrepreneurship: an empirical test of a truism. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 48(1), 29–36.

Risk Tolerance

  • Hvide & Panos (2014) use observed financial behaviour (owning individual stocks) as a revealed measure of risk tolerance for ~400,000 Norwegians. Stockholders are ~50% more likely to later start a firm.
  • But more risk tolerance ≠ better firms: risk-tolerant entrepreneurs start firms with ~25% lower sales and ~15% lower return on assets than firms started by the more risk-averse.
  • So risk tolerance predicts who enters — but if anything it predicts worse outcomes, not better ones.

Hvide, H. K., & Panos, G. A. (2014). Risk tolerance and entrepreneurship. Journal of Financial Economics, 111(1), 200–223.

Risk Tolerance

  • Levine & Rubinstein (2017) use a different proxy for risk tolerance: “illicit” behaviour as teenagers (damaging property, fighting, shoplifting, robbery, using force, assault or threats, drug use, dealing, gambling, being stopped by police, being charged).
  • Incorporated entrepreneurs (their proxy for growth orientation) are twice as likely as salaried workers to report having taken something by force as youths, ~40% more likely to have been stopped by police, and score 0.2 SD higher on an overall illicit-activity index.
  • The unincorporated also engage in more illicit activity than salaried workers — but the incorporated engage in still more.

Levine, R., & Rubinstein, Y. (2017). Smart and Illicit: Who Becomes an Entrepreneur and Do They Earn More? Quarterly Journal of Economics, 132(2), 963–1018.

Risk Tolerance — Kihlstrom & Laffont (1979)

  • Suppose people differ only in risk aversion r. Each chooses to be a worker or an entrepreneur.
  • Worker: earns the certain wage w → utility u(w).
  • Entrepreneur: hires labour L and runs a risky technology x = θ f(L) (f concave, shock θ, E[θ]=1). Profit π = θ f(L) − wL is uncertain.
  • You enter iff the risky venture beats the wage:
E[ u(π) ] ≥ u(w)
  • The risk premium grows with r, so there is a single cutoff r∗: people with r < r∗ become entrepreneurs, those with r > r∗ become workers.

Kihlstrom, R. E., & Laffont, J.-J. (1979). A general equilibrium entrepreneurial theory of firm formation based on risk aversion. Journal of Political Economy, 87(4), 719–748.

Risk Tolerance — Kihlstrom & Laffont (1979)

Fits the evidence Where K–L does well
  • Entrepreneurs are, in general, more risk-tolerant.
  • Innovation-driven / growth-oriented types are more risk-tolerant than subsistence types.
Hard to explain Where K–L struggles
  • More risk-tolerant entrepreneurs underperform the more risk-averse (Hvide & Panos, 2014).
  • No role for ability & skills, endogenous risk aversion (Camerer et al., 2002), or innovation — entrepreneurs are purely profit-driven.

Risk Tolerance — Kihlstrom & Laffont (1979)

  • An economy-wide rise in risk aversion (a pandemic, an oil crisis, …) lowers both the number of entrepreneurs and wages.
  • K–L assume entrepreneurs bear all the costs of failure. What if there is limited liability? Payoff becomes π̃ = max(θ f(L) − wL, 0) — losses beyond zero fall on others.
    • More risk-averse people now enter entrepreneurship (though this alone doesn’t say what they build).
    • Conditional on entering, they run larger firms and hire more: capping the downside removes the worst-case states that made scale costly, so the same r supports a bigger L.
    • Wages rise (more entrants = more labour demand), partly — but not fully — offsetting the scale effect.

The fine line of limited liability

  • Without limited liability, few people become entrepreneurs — and those who do may avoid “aiming big” to limit the risk of catastrophic personal losses.
  • With limited liability, however, entrepreneurs may take excessive risks if they believe they cannot be held personally accountable.

A taste for skewness: entrepreneurship as a lottery

  • Startup payoffs are right-skewed: most ventures fail or earn little; a few earn spectacular returns.
  • People routinely pay for positive skewness — buying lottery tickets and betting long shots even at negative expected value (the “favorite–long-shot bias”; Golec & Tamarkin 1998).
  • If founders value the small chance of a huge win, they will enter even when the median and mean returns are unattractive.

Golec, J., & Tamarkin, M. (1998). Bettors love skewness, not risk, at the horse track. Journal of Political Economy, 106(1), 205–225.

Explanation 3: Entrepreneurs are optimists

What optimism is
  • Optimism = the consistent belief that you are less likely than others to suffer bad events and more likely to experience good ones (Weinstein, 1980). “Good things will happen to you.”
  • It is strongest when (a) the event feels controllable and (b) there is commitment or emotional investment in the outcome.
  • Cooper, Woo & Dunkelberg (1988) surveyed ~3,000 new founders:
    • 81% put their own chances at 7-in-10 or better; 33% said a perfect 10-in-10.
    • Actual survival is far lower — and estimates for “a business like yours” dropped sharply.
The innovation-driven types
  • Bailey et al. (2026): a max optimism score of almost 20 (out of 24), vs 14.33 for the standard lab population (Scheier, Carver & Bridges, 1994).
  • Åstebro et al. (2007): independent inventors are significantly more optimistic than the general population.
    • 1/3 keep spending money and 1/2 keep spending time on projects after receiving highly diagnostic advice to cease.
    • The highly optimistic keep spending even after explicitly negative advice.

Cooper, A. C., Woo, C. Y., & Dunkelberg, W. C. (1988). Entrepreneurs’ perceived chances for success. Journal of Business Venturing, 3(2), 97–108.

Åstebro, T., Jeffrey, S. A., & Adomdza, G. K. (2007). Inventor perseverance after being told to quit: the role of cognitive biases. Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 20, 253–272.

Optimism has consequences

  • Landier & Thesmar (2009): French founders make systematic upward errors — they overestimate future sales and hiring.
  • More educated founders, and those with their own idea, are the most optimistic.
    • Those with better non-entrepreneurial outside options only enter when they are really optimistic (self-selection).
  • Optimists self-select into short-term debt: they expect to refinance on better terms soon, creating a separating equilibrium between optimists and realists.
  • Mild optimism may even help persistence — but it means expected returns are systematically overstated at entry.

Landier, A., & Thesmar, D. (2009). Financial contracting with optimistic entrepreneurs. The Review of Financial Studies, 22(1), 117–150.

Explanation 4: Overconfidence — three flavours

  • People enter because they subjectively perceive the return distribution too favorably when evaluating their own project.
  • Moore & Healy (2008) separate three biases that often get lumped together:
    • Over-estimation — thinking you (or your project) are better than you really are.
    • Over-placement — thinking you are better than others (“most startups fail, but not mine”).
    • Over-precision — being too certain your forecasts are right.

Moore, D. A., & Healy, P. J. (2008). The trouble with overconfidence. Psychological Review, 115(2), 502–517.

Overconfidence and excess entry

The core experiment
  • Camerer & Lovallo (1999): when payoffs depend on skill, people over-enter markets; when payoffs are random, entry is rational (over-placement).
    • Subjects entered a “market” where payoffs depended on their rank; in the skill condition, rank came from a trivia quiz.
  • “Reference-group neglect”: entrants focus on their own skill and forget they compete against other confident entrants.
Founders in the field
  • Busenitz & Barney (1997): founders are more overconfident than managers in large firms — equally likely to be wrong, far more sure they are right.
  • Koellinger, Minniti & Schade (2007): across 18 countries, subjective (often biased) confidence is one of the strongest correlates of starting a business.
  • Not all overconfidence causes excess entry: Herz et al. (2014) find over-precision leads to less experimentation (tunnel vision).

Camerer, C., & Lovallo, D. (1999). Overconfidence and excess entry: An experimental approach. American Economic Review, 89(1), 306–318. · Busenitz, L. W., & Barney, J. B. (1997). Journal of Business Venturing, 12(1), 9–30. · Koellinger, P., Minniti, M., & Schade, C. (2007). Journal of Economic Psychology, 28(4), 502–527.

What the Big Five say

  • Zhao & Seibert (2006), meta-analysis of entrepreneurs vs. managers. Entrepreneurs score:
  • Higher on Conscientiousness and Openness to experience.
  • Lower on Agreeableness and Neuroticism (i.e. more emotionally stable, more competitive).
  • No reliable difference on Extraversion.

Zhao, H., & Seibert, S. E. (2006). The Big Five personality dimensions and entrepreneurial status: A meta-analytical review. Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(2), 259–271. (Effects are small; magnitudes approximate.)

Traits predict entry better than success

  • Zhao, Seibert & Lumpkin (2010), meta-analysis: the Big Five relate to entrepreneurial intentions (multivariate R ≈ .36) somewhat more strongly than to entrepreneurial performance (R ≈ .31).
  • Some predictors of wanting to and becoming an entrepreneur (e.g. Agreeableness, risk propensity) do not predict how well the venture does.

Zhao, H., Seibert, S. E., & Lumpkin, G. T. (2010). The relationship of personality to entrepreneurial intentions and performance: A meta-analytic review. Journal of Management, 36(2), 381–404.

So how much do traits really explain?

  • Kerr, Kerr & Xu (2018) review the modern evidence: traits like internal locus of control, risk-taking, tolerance of ambiguity and overconfidence do matter — but effects are modest, and the field is full of disagreement, because “entrepreneurship” is so heterogeneous.
  • Gartner (1988): maybe we are asking the wrong question. “Who is an entrepreneur?” is the wrong question — entrepreneurship is not a personality type, it is an activity: creating an organization. Ask what entrepreneurs do, not who they are.

Kerr, S. P., Kerr, W. R., & Xu, T. (2018). Personality traits of entrepreneurs: A review of recent literature. Foundations and Trends in Entrepreneurship, 14(3), 279–356. · Gartner, W. B. (1988). “Who is an entrepreneur?” is the wrong question. American Journal of Small Business, 12(4), 11–32.

Skills, ability & human capital

Yes, I Can (?)

  • So far we explained the puzzle by saying entrepreneurs like the nature of the work.
  • These explanations mostly take returns as given / exogenous — e.g. Kihlstrom & Laffont (1979), where entrepreneurial performance is random.
  • But entrepreneurs can also influence their performance (why else the high internal locus of control?).
  • What if traits and skills impact performance? Low average returns may hide high returns for a skilled minority — the puzzle is about heterogeneity, not a universally bad deal.
Focus on two explanations
  • Entrepreneurship rewards ability more steeply than wage work
  • Entrepreneurs carry valuable, unique traits and skills

Explanation 5: Entrepreneurship rewards ability

  • People differ in ability a and sort into the occupation where their talent pays best — comparative advantage. Potential income rises with ability in each:
Employee:   wE(a) = μE + γE a Entrepreneur:   wS(a) = μS + γS a
  • Choice rule: become an entrepreneur iff wS(a) ≥ wE(a).
  • Two assumptions drive the result:
    • γS > γE — the entrepreneur is the residual claimant, keeping the marginal return to her talent (a wage compresses it). Ability is rewarded more steeply.
    • μS < μE — but she gives up the safe baseline wage, so at low ability entrepreneurship pays less.

Entrepreneurship rewards ability

  • The gain from entrepreneurship rises with ability: Δ(a) = (μS − μE) + (γS − γE) a.
  • Since γS > γE, there is a single ability threshold a∗ = (μE − μS) / (γS − γE): a > a∗ → entrepreneur, a < a∗ → employee ⇒ positive sorting on ability.
  • Predictions: the ablest self-select into entrepreneurship and earn more there; entrepreneurial earnings are more dispersed.

Entrepreneurship rewards ability

  • Levine & Rubinstein (2017): incorporated entrepreneurs have higher cognitive ability (AFQT) than employees and than unincorporated entrepreneurs.
  • Hegde & Tumlinson (2021): entrepreneurs have higher cognitive ability at each level of education (both incorporated and unincorporated).
  • But they do not find higher returns to cognitive ability for entrepreneurs.

Hegde, D., & Tumlinson, J. (2021). Information frictions and entrepreneurship. Strategic Management Journal, 42(3), 491–528.

Entrepreneurial Human Capital

  • These explanations treat ability as a one-dimensional “more is better.” But is human capital in entrepreneurship really that simple?
  • Ability is also usually treated as given — something you are born with. What about the human capital that can be “made”?

Are founders young dropouts?

  • The pervasive image: the twenty-something who dropped out of college to chase a startup dream and became an ultra-successful billionaire.
  • Yet most highly successful entrepreneurs are middle-aged (40–50), not young.
  • Most startups are founded by individuals with prior work experience (see next lecture).

Azoulay, P., Jones, B. F., Kim, J. D., & Miranda, J. (2020). Age and high-growth entrepreneurship. American Economic Review: Insights, 2(1), 65–82.

Does education pay for entrepreneurs?

  • Giorcelli (2024) studies the “Engineering, Science and Management War Training” (ESMWT) program — MBA-style education for middle managers and production supervisors at U.S. war industrial facilities in WWII.
  • ESMWT attendance systematically increased managers’ entrepreneurial activity. Effects were strongest for nonwhite and female participants — why?
  • Not just a signaling effect (“badge of honor”) — participation led to:
    • Better firm performance (less absenteeism, late orders, injuries, scrap) via adoption of managerial practices
    • Network building with managers of other facilities, raising the likelihood of co-founding a business together

Giorcelli, M. (2024). The Effects of Business School Education on Manager Career Outcomes.

Explanation 6: The ‘jack-of-all-trades’

  • Lazear (2004, 2005): running a firm needs a bit of everything — entrepreneurs are generalists, not specialists.
  • Balance beats depth: your earnings as a founder are limited by your weakest essential skill.
Employeew = max{x1, x2}
specializes — paid for their strongest skill
Entrepreneurπ = λ min{x1, x2}

Explanation 6: The ‘jack-of-all-trades’

  • People with more diverse education, prior work roles or fields are more likely to become (better) entrepreneurs.
  • Top management teams with more diverse experience have more success in accessing venture capital.

Innovation-driven entrepreneurship is a team sport

  • Traditional small business is mostly solo: the large majority have no employees and a single owner.
  • Innovation-driven ventures are typically team-founded — no one person holds all the specialized skills a differentiated product needs.
  • The founding team is how ventures satisfy Lazear’s balance requirement without every individual being a generalist.

Reconciling the puzzle

  • Low average returns are fully consistent with the evidence once we combine four things:
    • outcomes are right-skewed — most earn little, a few earn enormously
    • founders collect large non-pecuniary benefits (autonomy, meaning)
    • many enter on optimistic / overconfident beliefs that overstate their odds
    • a skilled minority genuinely earns high risk-adjusted returns (ability + human capital)
  • The puzzle is not “why does anyone do it?” — it is “averaging hides everything that matters.”

What this means for you

  • Heritable preferences and traits (risk attitude, personality) tilt the odds of entering (recall the nature share in Lindquist et al. / Vladasel et al.) — but there is no single “entrepreneur type.”
  • The things that most predict doing well — general and social ability, a balanced skill set, industry experience, a good team and network — are buildable (see also next class).
  • Beware your own optimism and overconfidence: they help you start, but can also let you persist with underperforming projects and ideas.
